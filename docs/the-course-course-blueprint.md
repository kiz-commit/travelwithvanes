# The Course Course

## Instructor blueprint — founding edition

### Core promise

Build a lean course worth putting in front of real customers. By the end, each student has a tested idea, a defined customer, a clear transformation, a minimum viable curriculum, an offer, a sales page, and a first-customer plan.

This is not a promise that every student will make money. It is a promise that they will leave with a specific, sellable first version and a responsible process for improving it.

### Positioning

**Hook:** Build a course worth buying — while taking this one.

**Belief:** Information is abundant. A useful path to a valued result is not.

**Difference:** Every lesson produces part of the student's real course. Students validate demand before committing to a polished production build.

**Teaching stance:** TravelwithVanes is a live case study, not proof of impossible wealth. Show the decisions, conversations, revisions, costs, and results honestly.

### Business principles underneath the course

These principles capture the Tiny MBA influence in original course language:

1. Value starts with another person's situation, not your content idea.
2. A small test that changes a decision is worth more than a large plan that changes nothing.
3. A product is useful only when it helps someone make progress.
4. Price, cost, time, and delivery are part of product design.
5. A business is a repeatable system for creating and capturing value.
6. Customer friction is information. Use it to improve the system.
7. Specific promises are easier to trust, teach, and sell than broad topics.

Do not use “Tiny MBA” in the public product name or imply affiliation. Credit it as one influence in an optional reading/resources note.

## Recommended founding format

- Six modules released across six weeks
- 30–45 minutes of teaching per module, split into short lessons
- One build workbook with editable templates
- One weekly group build session or office hour
- A small founding group so questions and sticking points can shape version two
- Students may sell a pilot before recording a polished course

The founding version can be delivered live with recordings. This reduces upfront production cost and makes the first cohort part of the research process.

## The student build board

| Stage | Student leaves with | Completion test |

| Idea | One course concept | Names a person, problem, and useful result |
| Student | Evidence-based student profile | Includes language from real conversations |

## Module 1 — Find your useful thing

### Outcome

The student chooses one course concept based on useful experience rather than a broad passion or trend.

### Lesson 1.1: Your unfair starting point

Teach students to inventory:

- Results they have created for themselves
- Processes they repeat in work or life
- Problems people already ask them to help solve
- Communities, industries, cultures, or situations they understand from the inside
- Mistakes they can help someone avoid

**Build:** Create a “proof I can help” inventory with at least ten entries.

### Lesson 1.2: Problem before topic

Contrast a topic with a valuable problem:

- Topic: travel photography
- Problem: my phone travel photos look flat and inconsistent
- Useful result: take and edit a coherent set of travel photos on a phone in seven days

**Build:** Turn the three strongest inventory items into person → problem → result statements.

### Lesson 1.3: Choose a course thesis

Score each concept from one to five for:

- Strength of the problem
- Access to potential students
- Evidence the student can help
- Speed of the result
- Willingness to teach it repeatedly

**Build:** Choose one concept and write: “I believe I can help [person] go from [problem] to [result] by teaching [path].”

### Gate 1

Do not progress with a concept described only as “everything about…” or one that cannot name a plausible student.

### TravelwithVanes case study

Move from “a course about UGC” to “help an early travel creator create a small portfolio of useful travel content they can confidently pitch to aligned brands.”

## Module 2 — Find the person who wants it

### Outcome

The student replaces assumptions with language and evidence from real potential customers.

### Lesson 2.1: Define the first student

Create a narrow starting profile based on situation and behavior, not demographics alone.

**Build:** Write a one-page first-student profile covering current situation, attempted solutions, constraints, desired progress, and where they already look for help.

### Lesson 2.2: Run the ten-person test

The conversation is research, not a disguised pitch. Ask:

1. What are you trying to achieve?
2. What is hardest about doing that now?
3. What have you already tried?
4. What happened?
5. What does this problem cost you in time, money, confidence, or missed opportunity?
6. What would a useful solution need to help you do?
7. Have you paid for help with this or a related problem before?
8. May I show you a first version when it is ready?

Avoid leading questions such as “Wouldn't a course about this be helpful?”

**Build:** Book ten conversations and keep a language bank of exact phrases, repeated problems, existing alternatives, and objections.

### Lesson 2.3: Read the signal

Teach the difference between compliments and evidence:

- Weak: “That sounds cool.”
- Better: a repeated, costly problem
- Stronger: prior attempts or spending
- Strongest: a deposit, preorder, or commitment to a pilot

**Build:** Write a short evidence report: continue, narrow, change, or stop—and why.

### Gate 2

Do not record the full course until the student has meaningful conversations and can explain what changed because of them.

### TravelwithVanes case study

Interview early travel creators about pitching, portfolio confidence, usage rights, finding contacts, and getting replies. Let their repeated problem determine the course promise.

## Module 3 — Design the transformation

### Outcome

The student creates a credible result and maps the decisions and actions needed to reach it.

### Lesson 3.1: Define A and B

Point A is the student's observable current state. Point B is an observable result the course can responsibly help create.

**Build:** Write a before-and-after statement using evidence from customer interviews.

### Lesson 3.2: Make the promise believable

Pressure-test the promise for:

- Specificity: can a student tell whether it happened?
- Relevance: does the result matter to the target student?
- Agency: can the course materially affect the outcome?
- Scope: can a first version teach it without pretending to control external results?
- Time and effort: is the path reasonable for this student?

Do not promise earnings, customers, employment, or other outcomes the instructor cannot control. Promise completed assets, capable actions, or demonstrated skills.

**Build:** Write the course promise and a plain-language disclaimer about what it does not guarantee.

### Lesson 3.3: Map the path backwards

Start at the result and ask:

1. What must the student do immediately before this?
2. What must they decide, understand, or have before that?
3. What mistakes or barriers are likely at each step?
4. What proof will show that the step is complete?

**Build:** Create the transformation map with milestones, barriers, and proof of completion.

### Gate 3

Every planned module must be necessary for the promised result. Interesting but nonessential material moves to a resource list or version two.

## Module 4 — Build the smallest version

### Outcome

The student turns the transformation map into the smallest course capable of helping the first students progress.

### Lesson 4.1: Curriculum as a sequence of actions

Use one module for each meaningful milestone. A useful lesson includes:

1. The decision or skill
2. A concise explanation
3. A relevant example
4. A student action
5. A completion check

**Build:** Create a course map with no more than six core modules for version one.

### Lesson 4.2: Choose the right teaching object

Not every idea needs a video. Choose between:

- Short demonstration video
- Written explanation
- Checklist
- Worksheet or template
- Worked example
- Live practice or feedback

**Build:** Assign the lightest useful format to each lesson.

### Lesson 4.3: Create the pilot

Teach the first version live or in a lightweight workspace before investing in a polished portal. Record only what should remain stable.

**Build:** Complete module one, the master workbook, and the schedule for delivering the rest.

### Gate 4

The first module must be teachable and the remaining path must be clear before the pilot is sold. The entire video library does not need to exist.

## Module 5 — Make it buyable

### Outcome

The student creates an economically sensible offer and a clear sales page.

### Lesson 5.1: Design the offer

Define:

- Core outcome
- Delivery format and access period
- Included tools and support
- Student effort and prerequisites
- Start date or immediate-access terms
- Refund and support boundaries

**Build:** Complete a one-page offer brief.

### Lesson 5.2: Price the first version

Consider four realities:

- Value: what useful progress is this helping create?
- Alternatives: what do students currently spend in money, time, or failed attempts?
- Delivery cost: tools, fees, support time, tax, and refunds
- Evidence: how much proof exists that this version works?

The founding price can reflect lower proof and higher access to the instructor. State why it is a founding price without using false countdowns or invented scarcity.

**Build:** Choose a founding price, calculate the contribution left after variable costs, and define the condition for raising the price.

### Lesson 5.3: Write the sales page

Use this order:

1. Specific outcome and intended student
2. Current problem and why common attempts stall
3. What the student will build
4. The path or curriculum
5. Delivery, support, price, and terms
6. Honest proof and case study
7. Fit and non-fit guidance
8. FAQs and clear call to action

**Build:** Publish a sales page that a potential student can respond to.

### Gate 5

A stranger should be able to explain who the course is for, what changes, what is included, what it costs, and what is not promised.

## Module 6 — Sell, learn, improve

### Outcome

The student has a credible plan to put the offer in front of the right people and improve it from use.

### Lesson 6.1: Find the first customers

Begin with channels that offer learning, not just reach:

- Follow up with research participants who asked to see the offer
- Personal outreach to relevant people
- Existing client or peer network
- A small live workshop
- Useful content answering the problems heard in interviews
- Aligned partnerships and communities, with permission

**Build:** List the first 30 appropriate people or places and select three acquisition actions for launch week.

### Lesson 6.2: Run a small launch

Use a simple sequence:

1. Name the problem
2. Teach one useful idea
3. Show the first version and who it is for
4. Answer real objections
5. Invite the right people to buy
6. Follow up without pressure

**Build:** Write the launch messages and schedule them.

### Lesson 6.3: Build the learning flywheel

Track where students:

- Do not start
- Pause or disappear
- Ask repeated questions
- Produce weak work
- Get a quick win
- Describe the value in their own words

Then improve in this order: confusing path, missing support, unnecessary work, weak examples, production polish.

**Build:** Create a weekly feedback form and version-two change log.

### Gate 6

The student can name how the offer reaches customers, how delivery creates the result, and how evidence changes the next version.

## Final student handoff

End the course with this inventory:

- You have a customer.
- You have a problem worth solving.
- You have a transformation you can responsibly teach.
- You have a small product.
- You have a price and sales page.
- You have a way to reach the first customers.
- You have a system for learning what to improve.

The closing action is not “congratulations, you finished.” It is: show the offer to the next right person.

## Founding-edition success measures

Do not judge the first edition only by gross sales. Track:

- Landing-page visitors who express interest
- Research conversations completed
- Interested people who enrol
- Students who complete each build gate
- Students who publish an offer
- Students who show the offer to a real potential customer
- Repeated questions and sticking points
- Refunds and reasons
- Support hours per student
- Student language that can improve the next sales page

The strongest early proof is not a revenue screenshot. It is evidence that students finish useful work and can explain how the course helped them move forward.
