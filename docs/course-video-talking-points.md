# TravelwithVanes course video talking points

## Recording approach

These videos are the human layer of the courses, not the course itself. The written lesson should still make sense without pressing play.

- Aim for **4–8 minutes** per video.
- Speak from bullet points rather than memorising a script.
- Record vertically or horizontally, but use the same orientation for a whole course. Horizontal 16:9 will fit the current lesson player best.
- Use a phone, window light, a quiet room, and a simple background connected to Vanessa's real life: desk, kitchen table, parked van, campsite, or travel-planning setup.
- Keep one imperfect personal detail when it adds warmth. Remove rambling, repeated explanations, and long introductions.
- Open with the point, then introduce the lesson. Avoid “Hey guys, welcome back…” unless it feels natural.
- End by naming the action directly below the video. Do not add a second generic call to action.
- Avoid income promises, investment recommendations, or implying that Vanessa's result will be typical for everyone.

Suggested reusable structure:

1. A personal or surprising opening sentence — 15 seconds
2. Why this lesson matters — 30 seconds
3. Two or three teaching points — 3–5 minutes
4. One real example from Vanessa — 1 minute
5. The student's next action — 20 seconds

# Course 1: The Van Fund

The Van Fund has three short videos across five lessons. Lessons 2 and 4 remain written because their information is easier to reference and carries less risk of sounding like personal financial advice.

## Video VF-01 — The number the dream reel leaves out

**Lesson:** 01 — Price the whole road  
**Target length:** 4–5 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Welcome students and replace the exciting listing price with the idea of an all-in travel target.

### Opening options

- “The price of the van is not the price of van life.”
- “The cheapest number in the plan is often the one in the listing.”
- “I love a spontaneous road trip. I do not love a spontaneous repair bill.”

### Talking points

- Briefly introduce Vanessa as a Brazilian traveller based in Australia and explain why the road is emotional as well as practical.
- Describe a moment when a trip or vehicle cost more than the attractive first number suggested.
- Walk through the four cost groups without quoting universal figures:
  - buying the vehicle;
  - making it safe and usable;
  - paying for the actual trip;
  - keeping a separate van-specific surprise buffer.
- Explain why a real quote or local fee is more useful than a creator's spending recap from a different year, route, vehicle, or country.
- Show the difference between an everyday emergency fund and a van-specific contingency, without prescribing the size of either.
- Frame the calculator as a way to test assumptions, not predict the future.
- Demonstrate how travel duration changes the trip total, then compare the Budget-led, Vanes pace, and More comfort examples.
- Show that every monthly category is editable and that students with an existing route budget can switch to a custom total.
- Add expected travel income only as a conservative usable estimate. Explain that uncertain sales should stay at zero and that gross income may not be available after work costs and tax.

### Personal example to prepare

Bring one real receipt, quote, listing, or spreadsheet line that surprised Vanessa. Hide personal details. Explain what changed in the plan after seeing it.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- Scrolling through three different van listings
- A handwritten four-column cost page
- Fuel, registration, service, or campsite receipts
- Entering a travel duration, editing the Vanes pace preset, and testing expected income in the Van Fund calculator

### Closing line

“Your job now is to price your road—not copy mine. Research the four groups, use the calculator, and notice which number changes the dream.”

## Video VF-02 — My low-drama payday routine

**Lesson:** 03 — Build your Van Fund  
**Target length:** 4–6 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Make the savings system feel ordinary, repeatable, and emotionally realistic.

### Opening options

- “The best savings routine is a little boring.”
- “If I have to feel motivated every payday, the system is already broken.”

### Talking points

- Explain that Vanessa is sharing a behavioural routine, not recommending a bank account, savings rate, or financial product.
- Describe why a named, separate account makes a goal more visible and spending it slightly harder.
- Explain the three-part rhythm:
  - separate the goal;
  - automate a sustainable amount when income arrives;
  - sweep an agreed share of unexpected income into the goal.
- Talk honestly about starting below the maximum amount so the plan survives an ordinary month.
- Explain the difference between checking progress monthly and checking obsessively.
- Mention that bonus-rate conditions, fees, access rules, and interest rates need to be checked by the student using current information.

### Personal example to prepare

Show a fictionalised payday split or use round example numbers. Explain how Vanessa would reduce the transfer during an expensive month rather than abandon the entire system.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- Renaming a demo savings account “Van Fund”
- Adding a recurring calendar reminder
- A five-minute monthly check-in at a desk
- Moving a small round-number amount in a mock banking screen

### Closing line

“Choose the amount that can survive real life, schedule it, and let the system do more work than your mood.”

## Video VF-03 — Funding freedom without hating the present

**Lesson:** 05 — Close the gap gently  
**Target length:** 5–6 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Close the course with perspective and help students choose sustainable cost and income experiments.

### Opening options

- “A future trip should not make you hate every Tuesday before it.”
- “There are two levers—spending and income—but neither needs to become your whole personality.”

### Talking points

- Acknowledge that cutting costs has a limit and can create shame or burnout when treated as the only answer.
- Explain the idea of one gentle move on each side:
  - remove or renegotiate one low-value cost;
  - run one small, defined income experiment.
- Give examples relevant to Vanessa's real experience: a UGC package, guide, freelance task, casual shift, or teaching one useful result.
- Define an experiment using one buyer, one result, one delivery, and one review date.
- Encourage tracking time and costs as well as money earned.
- Mention that extra income may create tax and record-keeping responsibilities; students should check current ATO guidance or ask a registered professional.
- Reframe a later departure date as a planning decision, not failure.

### Personal example to prepare

Choose one small project Vanessa has tried. Share what made it repeatable or not repeatable without presenting the result as typical.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- Cancelling an unused subscription
- Creating a one-page offer
- Filming a simple piece of UGC
- Updating the Van Fund total

### Closing line

“Pick the version of this plan you could still respect six months from now. That is the one most likely to take you somewhere.”

# Course 2: The Course Course

The Course Course has four videos across six lessons. Lessons 3 and 5 are writing-heavy by design because students need the promises, pricing assumptions, and offer terms in a form they can reread carefully.

## Video CC-01 — You probably know something useful already

**Lesson:** 01 — Find your useful thing  
**Target length:** 5–7 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Remove the pressure to invent a trendy course idea and demonstrate the proof inventory.

### Opening options

- “You do not need a course idea yet. You need evidence that you can help.”
- “The useful thing is often so normal to you that you stopped noticing it.”

### Talking points

- Explain the difference between feeling like an expert and having evidence of helping someone make progress.
- Walk through the five inventory sources:
  - results Vanessa has created;
  - repeatable processes;
  - questions people ask her;
  - communities or situations she knows from inside;
  - mistakes she can help another person avoid.
- Share the shift from “a course about UGC” to a result such as helping an early travel creator make a small portfolio they can confidently pitch.
- Contrast a broad topic with a person → problem → result statement.
- Explain why willingness to teach the same path repeatedly belongs in the scoring process.
- Encourage students to keep ideas small enough to test.

### Demonstration to prepare

Bring a real list of ten “proof I can help” entries. Circle three and speak through why one has stronger evidence or access to potential students.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- Handwritten inventory page
- DMs with names and details hidden
- Old work, templates, guides, or repeated checklists
- Scoring three ideas from one to five

### Closing line

“Do not choose the idea that looks most impressive. Choose the useful result you have the strongest reason to test.”

## Video CC-02 — How to research without secretly pitching

**Lesson:** 02 — Find the person who wants it  
**Target length:** 6–8 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Model a natural research conversation and teach students to hear evidence rather than fish for encouragement.

### Opening options

- “If your research question already contains the answer you want, it is not research.”
- “Someone saying ‘I love that idea’ is lovely. It is not a buying signal.”

### Talking points

- Explain that the purpose of the conversation is to change or sharpen a decision.
- Show how to ask about the person's current goal, difficulty, attempted solutions, result, and cost of the problem.
- Explain why “Would you buy a course about this?” is too hypothetical.
- Demonstrate one leading question and rewrite it as a neutral question.
- Explain the evidence ladder:
  - compliment;
  - repeated problem;
  - prior attempt or spending;
  - request to see the solution;
  - deposit, preorder, or pilot commitment.
- Show how to capture exact phrases without turning one comment into a universal truth.
- Give students permission to narrow, change, or stop the idea after research.

### Demonstration to prepare

Record a two-minute mock conversation with a friend or show Vanessa answering both sides. Include one follow-up question: “Can you tell me about the last time that happened?”

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- A simple interview question sheet
- Highlighting repeated phrases in notes
- A language-bank table
- Four cards labelled continue, narrow, change, stop

### Closing line

“Book the conversations to learn, not to be told you are right. The best result is a clearer decision.”

## Video CC-03 — What deserves a video—and what does not

**Lesson:** 04 — Build the smallest useful version  
**Target length:** 5–7 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Demonstrate lightweight instructional design and model the same video restraint used in these courses.

### Opening options

- “A video is not automatically more valuable because it took longer to make.”
- “The format should do a job. If it does not, use the lighter option.”

### Talking points

- Name the jobs video does well: demonstration, emotion, nuance, judgement, and personal context.
- Name the jobs writing does well: reference, precision, scanning, copying, and revisiting.
- Explain where checklists, templates, worked examples, and live feedback are stronger.
- Use one sample lesson and choose the format for each part.
- Point out that The Course Course itself uses only four videos across six modules.
- Explain the anatomy of a useful lesson:
  - decision or skill;
  - concise teaching;
  - example;
  - action;
  - completion check.
- Encourage a live or lightweight pilot before a polished production build.

### Demonstration to prepare

Take a hypothetical lesson called “Write your course promise.” Sort its parts into a short intro video, written pressure test, template, worked example, and feedback task.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- Format cards being arranged on a table
- A six-module course map
- A rough phone setup beside a written worksheet
- Deleting an unnecessary planned video from the outline

### Closing line

“Assign the lightest format that helps the student move. Production is allowed to be smaller than your ambition.”

## Video CC-04 — The first launch without guru theatre

**Lesson:** 06 — Sell, learn, improve  
**Target length:** 6–8 minutes  
**Role of the video:** Turn launching into a grounded invitation and feedback loop rather than a performance.

### Opening options

- “Your first launch does not need a countdown clock. It needs the right people to understand the offer.”
- “The first launch is not a verdict on you. It is a test of the current offer.”

### Talking points

- Start with people and places that create learning: research participants, relevant contacts, clients, peers, a small workshop, useful content, and aligned communities where participation is welcome.
- Explain why a list of 30 appropriate people or places is more useful than “post more.”
- Walk through the simple launch sequence:
  - name the real problem;
  - teach one useful idea;
  - show the first version and who it is for;
  - answer real objections;
  - invite the right people;
  - follow up without pressure.
- Distinguish an honest invitation from urgency tactics, invented scarcity, or income claims.
- Explain what to observe during delivery: non-starts, pauses, repeated questions, weak work, quick wins, and student language.
- Prioritise improvements in this order: confusing path, missing support, unnecessary work, weak examples, and finally production polish.
- Close by making “show it to the next right person” the graduation action.

### Personal example to prepare

Share a real launch, post, pitch, or offer Vanessa changed after a response or lack of response. Focus on the decision learned, not a revenue figure.

### Natural visuals / B-roll

- A first-30 map with private details hidden
- Drafting a short invitation message
- A feedback form
- A version-two change log

### Closing line

“You do not graduate by perfecting the offer in private. You graduate by showing the useful first version to the next right person.”

# Upload and publishing checklist

For each finished video:

- Export at 1080p with clear spoken audio.
- Add accurate captions before publishing.
- Use a simple thumbnail or poster image with no more than five words.
- Name files consistently, for example `van-fund-01-whole-road.mp4`.
- Upload the video to the site's media storage or a supported host.
- Add the final URL to the matching `videoUrl` field in `src/lib/course-content.ts`.
- Add a `posterUrl` beside it if using a directly hosted MP4.
- Test playback on desktop and mobile.
- Keep the written lesson complete even after the video is live.

The lesson player accepts direct video files, YouTube links, and Vimeo links.
